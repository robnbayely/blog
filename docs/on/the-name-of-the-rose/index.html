The name of the rose

and the big ball of mud

Sunday 23rd November 2025 | 6 minute readSun 23rd Nov 2025 | 6 minute read

Naming things is hard. So hard, in fact, that it is considered (by programmers) to be one of the two hardest problems in computer science. Lots of people (programmers) agree on this. Ask anyone (programmers) what they think the hardest thing in computer science is. Naming things, is what they'll say.

On the surface it might seem like this is true. Indeed, all but the very simplest software requires a dizzying myriad of variables, functions, classes, commands, constants, arguments, packages and namespaces to be written into existence in total lexicographic harmony.

Advice on the matter is curiously reminiscent of dialogue from Catch-22. Identifiers, they'll say, should be:

  • really descriptive and meaningful so that software archaeologists in a hundred years' time will know precisely what was intended, with a healthy amount of of units and types and actions and nouns and so forth;

  • concise and compact because other programmers are always very busy and don't have time to actually read your code;

  • don't use abbreviations, thank you very much;

  • ... except, of course, for HTTP, API, REST, ASCII, A11Y, I18N, CRUD, SDK, XML, TCP, YAML, or Y2K, for example — never, ever un-abbreviate those ones;

  • always keep your camels separate from your kebabs and your snakes;

  • above all be perfectly consistent, except make it totally unique because otherwise there will be a namespace collision.

William Shakespeare (not a programmer) thought that naming things was easy.

What’s in a name? That which we call a rose
By any other name would smell as sweet;

The Tragedy of Romeo and Juliet

Described as "the most influential lexical originator in the history of English", Shakespeare is directly attributed with inventing almost two thousand words.

Far be it from anyone to argue with The Bard, however, commenting on studies from Oxford University's crossmodal laboratory, prominent gastrophysicists note that the speech sounds that are present in the brand names of alcoholic beverages can "almost subliminally set taste expectations".

From Wageningen University in the Netherlands What’s in a name? The effect of congruent and incongruent product names on liking and emotions when consuming beer or non-alcoholic beer in a bar relates that the mere labeling of actual beer as non-alcoholic beer emphatically tanked participants' ratings of the experience, feeling overall less comforted or exuberant, and disappointed.

A study from 1924, Ein experimenteller Beitrag zum Problem der psychologischen Grundlagen der Namengebung, describes an experiment in which participants were shown a series of drawings and asked to select from a list of nonsense words the word they associated with or felt most closely resembled the image. In what has become known as the takete-maluma or bouba-kiki phenomenon, the significant overlap between participants' responses implies certain regularities "which the human soul follows in the process of name-giving".

Philosophically speaking, the debate over whether words have an intrinsic relation to the things they signify versus an arbitrary value determined by convention has been going back and forth for several thousand years. In the 13th century, William of Ockham, argued that universals (like names or categories) do not have an independent existence apart from the objects they refer to, in opposition to Platonic realism. Writing in the 20th century, Ludwig Wittgenstein explored how the meaning of a word is deeply tied to its use in language, emphasising that context determines the meaning of words, and that meaning is therefore contingent on social interaction, or convention. In Phenomenology, Edmund Husserl proposed that objects exist independently of the perceiving subject, but that our understanding of them is mediated by consciousness, whereby experiences shape the perception of those objects.

Perhaps the greatest mathematician of the 20th century, Alexander Grothendieck, revolutionised algebraic geometry using unconventional conventions, naming objects in informative yet evocative ways which reflected sometimes deeply personal perspectives.

Grothendieck had a taste and a talent for naming things, which he used as a major intellectual strategy ... He had a special talent for naming things before possessing and conquering them, and many of his terminological choices were remarkable. A Country Known Only by Name

The idea that possession of (or control over) a thing could be somehow conferred by the very act of naming is an ancient concept that exists across a number of varied cultures and mythologies. In a folk tale that is four thousand years old, the life of the queen's firstborn is saved when she learns the name of her antagonist, Rumpelstiltskin.

Without names there is nothing. If the mountain has no name, I did not climb it. I have not seen the painting without a name, not read the book without a title. Only the gymnastic skills with a name can be performed: cartwheel, straddle jump, cross split, Suzuki. That radical dependency on names is expressed in the Rumpelstiltskin fairy tale ... If she cannot guess the name, the girl, in the meantime queen, will lose her baby. Rumpelstiltskin: the magic of the right word

In every aspect, names carry identity: the relation each thing bears only to itself. For humans, our identity is the set of characteristics that define us as individual. In programming, it is the fundamental property that identifies something uniquely. To name something is to have participated in a process of discovery, to have identified that by which it is distinct from other things.

What is a thing but a name? To really name something you have to know what it is, and that is harder than you think.

Ask HN: Why is naming things in programming so hard?

The power of words, the nature of identity and the essence of being; these themes have resonated across antiquity, folklore, mysticism, science, philosophy, and software.

Metaphorical thinking is pervasive in software development ... Software designs tend to be very abstract and hard to grasp. Developers and users alike need tangible ways to understand the system and share a view of the system as a whole. Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software

Naming things is hard without a shared understanding or common vocabulary. Context is material when drawing the boundaries between identities, and context is defined by collective discovery. When we fail to seek a ubiquitous language through the process of collaborative identification, contexts are unknown or unclear, characteristics remain indistinct, and names elude us.

And when that happens, what we get, metaphorically speaking, is not a rose, but a big ball of mud.
